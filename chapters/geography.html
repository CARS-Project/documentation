7  Geography: postcode areas

The only location in the CARS data is the postcode area (PCA) of the testing station where the vehicle had its MOT. This chapter explains what postcode areas are, how they relate to other geographies, and the limits of analysing vehicles at this scale.

7.1 Postcodes and postcode areas

Postcodes were first used in London in the 1850s and 1860s, with districts named by compass point (N, E, SW and so on), and later extended to some large provincial cities. The current national system was rolled out between the 1950s and the 1970s. Postcodes are designed by Royal Mail for one purpose: to sort and deliver mail efficiently.

A full postcode such as LS2 9JT has four nested levels:

Level Example Number in the UK (approx.)
Area LS (Leeds) about 120
District LS2 about 3,000
Sector LS2 9 about 11,000
Unit LS2 9JT about 1.8 million

The area is the one or two letters at the start, usually from the name of the main town or city: LS for Leeds, B for Birmingham, G for Glasgow, L for Liverpool. The CARS data has 119 postcode areas. In the data, Northern Ireland is coded NI rather than its real postcode area BT, and a small number of vehicles have the code XX, which is not a real area.

7.2 How postcode areas differ from other geographies

Postcode areas are large and very uneven. In 2022 the largest held 1,178,874 observed cars (Birmingham) and the smallest 13,012 (Lerwick). They mix cities with their rural hinterlands, and they do not line up with the geographies most other statistics use:

  • Census geographies (output areas, nested in LSOAs, nested in MSOAs in England and Wales, with equivalents elsewhere) are designed to hold populations of similar size and character.
  • Administrative geographies (wards, local authority districts, counties and regions) are the units of local government and policy.
  • Travel to work areas (228 in the UK) are built from commuting flows.

Because postcode boundaries do not follow any of these, linking postcode area data to a local authority or a census area is always a best fit. The usual method is to assign each unit postcode to the area containing its centroid, using the ONS Postcode Directory, and then to aggregate. Some addresses, and so some vehicles, inevitably fall in the “wrong” area.

7.3 The limits of analysis by postcode area

Where vehicles are tested is not where they are kept

The postcode area is that of the testing station, not the keeper’s address. For most private vehicles the two are close, but:

  • vehicles kept near a postcode area boundary may be tested on either side of it;
  • people may have their vehicle tested near work rather than home;
  • fleet and company vehicles may be tested wherever the fleet is maintained;
  • a vehicle tested in a different area from last year has not necessarily moved: it may simply have gone to a different garage.

The CARS churn tables count a change of area between years as a move, and should be read with this in mind.

The modifiable areal unit problem

Any analysis of data aggregated to areas is affected by the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP). It has two parts:

  1. The scale effect. Results change with the size of the areas. Average car mileage or vehicle ownership calculated for small areas such as LSOAs varies far more than the same measure calculated for postcode areas, because the large areas average together places that are very different. Low-mileage city centres disappear when they are combined with their higher-mileage hinterlands.
  2. The zoning effect. Different ways of drawing areas of the same size from the same small units can give different results, because any particular set of boundaries is arbitrary. Postcode areas, drawn for mail delivery, have no reason to separate places with different travel behaviour.

Aggregation also brings the ecological fallacy: a relationship seen between areas need not hold for the people or vehicles within them, and can even be reversed. It is easy to find correlations between area averages that are negligible at the level of individual vehicles.

ImportantPitfall: within-area variation can exceed between-area variation

Postcode areas give reliable counts, because they are large. But the differences between areas can be smaller than the differences within them, so an area average may describe very few of the vehicles in it. Use postcode area results to compare places at a broad scale, not to describe neighbourhoods.

Do patterns between areas mean anything?

One check on whether area-level differences are signal rather than noise is spatial autocorrelation: whether neighbouring areas have more similar values than distant ones. An early analysis of car mileage by postcode area found some, but limited, clustering. The clearest cluster is London, where mileage is low, which confirms that the data behaves as expected but is not in itself a new finding.
